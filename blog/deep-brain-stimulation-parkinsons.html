Deep Brain Stimulation for Parkinson's: The Basics

Deep brain stimulation can ease Parkinson's tremor, stiffness, and slowness. Learn how DBS works, who it may help, and what to expect, in plain English.

By the Palm Beach Neurology team · July 2026 · Medically reviewed by Dr. Paul Winner, DO, FAAN

If you or someone you love is living with Parkinson's disease, you may have heard the term "deep brain stimulation," or DBS. It can sound intimidating at first, but the idea behind it is fairly straightforward. This article walks through the basics in plain English so you can have a more informed conversation with your neurologist.

What Is Deep Brain Stimulation?

Deep brain stimulation is a treatment that uses gentle electrical impulses to help regulate the brain signals involved in movement. During a procedure performed by a neurosurgeon, thin wires called leads are placed in specific areas of the brain that help control motor function. These leads connect to a small device, similar in concept to a heart pacemaker, that sits under the skin near the collarbone. The device sends carefully tuned signals that can help calm some of the movement problems Parkinson's causes.

DBS is not a cure, and it does not stop Parkinson's from progressing. Instead, it is a tool that can help manage symptoms and, for the right person, improve day-to-day quality of life.

Which Symptoms Can It Help?

DBS is best known for easing the movement-related symptoms of Parkinson's, including tremor, stiffness, and slowness of movement. For many people, it can also reduce the involuntary movements, called dyskinesia, that sometimes come with long-term Parkinson's medication. In some cases, DBS allows a person to lower their medication dose, which may cut down on side effects.

It is worth knowing that DBS tends to help symptoms that already respond, at least somewhat, to medication. Symptoms that do not improve with medication are less likely to change with stimulation.

Who Might Be a Candidate?

DBS is generally considered for people whose Parkinson's symptoms are no longer well controlled by medication alone, or who struggle with medication side effects or symptoms that come and go throughout the day. It is not the right first step for everyone, and it is usually not the first treatment tried. Deciding whether DBS makes sense involves a careful evaluation by a movement-disorders specialist, who will weigh your symptoms, overall health, and personal goals.

What Happens After the Device Is Placed?

The surgery is only part of the process. After the device is implanted and you have had time to heal, a neurologist begins "programming" it, fine-tuning the strength and pattern of the electrical signals to match your needs. This is done in the office, is adjustable, and often takes several visits to get right. Your settings can be updated over time as your symptoms change. Every person's Parkinson's journey is different, and the decision about whether DBS is a good fit is a personal one made together with your care team.
